# Source examples

Examples from *Addicting Storytelling* (HRG deck and workshop) and the user-supplied launch article. PDF page numbers start at 1; talk times are elapsed minutes. Quotes retain source wording apart from punctuation, line breaks, and capitalization. “Stronger” means the source's preferred version, not a universal wording rule.

## Workshop: weak → strong

| Technique | Weaker source version | Stronger source version | Transferable lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| A consequence carries the message (PDF 6–7) | “Don't wander off at night. It's dangerous.” | “There was once a child who wandered off at night. He was eaten by a saber tooth tiger.” | Let an event make the stakes felt. This is an illustrative tale. |
| Clear, unexpected framing (PDF 23) | “Is music hacking your brain?” | “Is music a drug?” | A short, familiar analogy can open a sharper question. |
| Concrete experience (PDF 37–38) | “We all love music because it sounds nice.” | “We all love music because it can turn any boring moment into a freaking movie.” | Replace bland evaluation with an experience the audience can picture. |
| Actual tension (PDF 24) | “But that's the problem.” | “But you end up turning EVERY boring moment into a movie.” | Name the reversal instead of announcing that one exists. |
| Specific action (PDF 25) | “Listen to this music. Because this is what you choreograph your life to.” | “So the next time you leave the house, leave the AirPods.” | Give the audience something concrete to do. |

The revised ending adds: “And you'll realize that the best music is the melody of your own thoughts.” Its payoff returns to the music metaphor and resolves the argument for silence (PDF 25).

## Causal skeleton

The deck contrasts **beat → and then → beat → and then → beat** with **beat → but → beat → therefore → beat** (PDF 52–53).

Its music story moves through **appeal → hidden cost → consequence → value of silence → action → thematic payoff** (PDF 55–56). That skeleton is our distillation, not a separately named source framework.

## What the talk adds

- **Make value tangible:** “a thousand songs in your pocket” illustrates a short, visualizable product promise (04:20–04:46; the speaker attributes it to Steve Jobs).
- **Close the knowledge gap:** explain from the audience's context; expertise makes missing context invisible to the author (11:49–13:34).
- **Withhold the answer, not orientation:** give enough information to make the question worth pursuing, then use breadcrumbs to sustain interest (17:56–19:09).
- **Visual stories still need one idea:** the “intention” example uses a flurry of images, a clock/alarm peak, then a calmer ending. The speaker emphasizes letting viewers pause long enough to process a significant moment (24:13–26:43).
- **Keep authorship:** AI can help find starting ideas; the finished script should retain the storyteller's voice (28:57–29:14).
- **Build connection:** relevant vulnerability and failure can make a story relatable; begin with a struggle the audience recognizes (30:08–30:42).
- **Serve the purpose:** the speaker distinguishes reach from an audience that trusts your expertise and may buy your products (39:15–40:17). Apply the principle without adopting his speculative income examples.

## Launch article: claims that travel

Source: user-pasted *The Five Layers Behind an “Everywhere” Launch*; author and URL were not supplied. Section names below identify its layers.

| Weaker | Stronger | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| “An AI-powered platform for collaborative application development.” | “Build an app by describing it.” | State a repeatable change in what someone can do (Layer 1). |
| Logo animation → founder introduction → dashboard tour → eventual result. | One meaningful result in the opening five seconds, clear without sound or prior context. | Let the demo make the claim believable (Layer 2; paraphrased comparison). |
| Many accounts repeat identical copy. | Different people explain the same core idea through their own experience and perspective. | Keep the claim consistent and the voices distinct (Layer 3; paraphrased comparison). |

**Source formula:** familiar problem + surprising claim + visible proof. The invitation in the skill's marketing pattern is our extension.

**Opening test:** show a short demo's first five seconds to an outsider. Can they explain what happened and why it matters? Early proof can open curiosity about the mechanism; it need not reveal every later consequence.

**Beyond one telling:** Layers 4–5 describe timed waves and an audience that starts sharing independently. The transferable lesson is to build on prior attention with fresh evidence or perspectives, then learn from genuine reactions. Posting schedules and launch staffing remain outside this skill. Coordination supplies distribution, not independent corroboration; audience advocacy must be earned.

## Source boundaries

- The workshop compares 20.9K and 1.3M views, describing the revision as roughly 60× better with the same footage and editing (PDF 20–22). This is a presenter-reported comparison, not a controlled test or a forecast.
- “Drug,” “addicted,” and “conditions your brain to hate silence” illustrate charged language; these materials do not establish the implied medical claims. Preserve the vividness technique while grounding new stories in supported facts.
- Treat the peak-end rule as a craft heuristic here, not a guarantee of audience memory.
- The speaker attributes SUCCESs to *Made to Stick* (10:41–11:11). Three-act structure receives only “beginning, middle, and end” (17:45–17:51); the setup/conflict/change expansion in the skill is an adaptation.
